ABSTRACT
The detection of microseismic noise generated by survivors trapped by debris is a method already used
by Search and Rescue (S&R) teams. Present seismic S&R equipment works exclusively on energy
analysis whilst ignoring information associated with propagation delays. We explore the potential of
using traveltime analysis compared to energy analysis for both 2D and 3D location. Results obtained
from three different debris field scenarios used for training S&R teams demonstrate that using travel-
time information is more reliable than using energy information alone. A joint analysis of both signal
parameters  is  suggested  as  an  appropriate  strategy  to  improve  the  reliability  of  locating  survivors.
Traveltimes can also potentially extend the location into the third dimension by returning an approxi-
mate estimate of the survivor depth below ground level. The main obstacles to achieving this goal are
the inhomogeneity of the debris pile, the need for a real-time response and the limited spatial extension
of the sensor array. Despite these difficulties, results obtained in the field, with an algorithm based on
energy focusing by means of cross-correlation and semblance operators, show an accuracy within the
limit  of  the  seismic  resolution. A  new  searching  strategy  is  defined  and  the  procedure  reduces  the
investigation time taken by current seismic S&R systems by a factor of three.

INTRODUCTION
Both natural and human-triggered catastrophes can cause people
to  be  trapped  under  rubble.  Increased  hydrogeological  hazards
due to global climatic changes, earthquakes, mine collapses and
terrorist  attacks  may  give  rise  to  situations  where  robust  and
timely  Search  and  Rescue  (S&R)  operations  need  to  be  per-
formed (FEMA 2000). Worldwide, S&R teams rely on conven-
tional  search  strategies  such  as  highly-trained  search  dogs,
physical void inspection and audible call out/knocking; but they
are also testing instruments equipped with optical, thermal, elec-
tromagnetic and seismo-acoustic sensing elements. Canine units
are still the most flexible and helpful tool in the rescuer’s arsenal
since  they  can  move  rapidly  across  debris  piles  and  their  per-
formance is not limited by natural and anthropic environmental
noise (FEMA 2000). Yet, the human scent they rely on to locate
a  possible  survivor  may  have  travelled  an  intricate  path  in  the
subsurface  and  a  long  distance  from  the  actual  victim.  S&R
cameras are passive devices that may work in different frequency
bands, from the infrared to the visible spectrum (FEMA 2000).
They  are  small  and  feature  either  telescopic  or  cable-based
probes that feed down through the voids and inspect the debris
from  the  inside.  However,  such  operations  are  time-consuming

  Shallow geophysical techniques have recently been employed
to provide successful S&R systems and methodologies but elas-
tic as well as electromagnetic investigations face common criti-
cal  issues.  Microwave  investigation  may  be  a  suitable  solution
when  the  survivor  is  completely  trapped,  unable  to  move,  or
unconscious. The physical principles of this approach are simple
and based on the fact that electromagnetic waves can penetrate a
rubble pile, illuminate a victim and be modulated by his or her
movements,  including  breathing  and  heartbeat  (Chen  et  al.
2000).  At  present,  hardware  and  software  developments  are
increasing with the ground-penetrating radar (GPR) community
(Pieraccini  et  al.  2008;  Cist  2009)  being  active  in  this  field.
Researchers are addressing the reduction of clutter, (i.e., the high
energy  reflections  produced  from  the  stationary  background
material that overwhelms the weak reflected signals from a bur-
ied  person).  Additional  shortcomings  are  associated  with  fea-
tures  detected  behind  the  antenna  (despite  shielding),  interfer-
ence from other microwave devices, the presence of metal rebars
and  conductive  materials  and  the  achievable  penetration  depth,
according to the frequency of radiation.

  A  key  issue  for  an  efficient  microseismic  monitoring  study
relates  to  the  source  location  schemes.  Determining  the  spatial
coordinates of a seismic event has been a research topic for sev-
eral decades and a considerable volume of published literature is
available  (Ge  2003a,b).  In  the  geophysical  field,  several
approaches  have  addressed  earthquake  seismology  (Geiger
1912; Lomax et al. 2000; Husen et al. 2003) and recent applica-
tions  to  the  hydrocarbon  industry  have  supported  the  develop-
ment of refined location methods, oriented to the evaluation of
accuracy, computational costs and suitability for real-time opera-
tions (Oye and Roth 2003; Gajewski and Tessmer 2005; Rentsch
et  al.  2007;  Vesnaver  et  al.  2008;  Eisner  et  al.  2009,  2008).
Current location procedures may be grouped into i) arrival-time-
based algorithms, based on the identification of seismic phases at
the receivers and on the inversion of the picked arrival times and
ii)  migration-based  techniques  that  exploit  the  full  waveform
recorded and back-propagate collected energy from the receivers
into the actual hypocenter (also known as energy-focusing tech-
niques). Besides sensor array geometry and size, errors in trans-
ducer coordinates and the position of the source relative to the
array, the factors that primarily affect source location accuracy,
are the goodness of the velocity model utilized and the accuracy
of the arrival time picking (whenever an arrival-time-based algo-
rithm  is  used).  S&R  scenarios  represent  a  very  challenging
application for source location algorithm as they exhibit a high
degree of background noise, inhomogeneous propagation condi-
tions, significant dispersion and the simultaneous arrival of dif-
ferent wave types along with poor sensor coupling. The need for
a real-time and accurate response, despite a very poor frequency
content of the signals, is a further key point.
  As far as the author is aware, there has been no application of
high-resolution shallow seismic techniques to S&R problems in
the recent scientific literature, apart from the work by Hanafy et
al.  (2009).  They  described  the  location  of  miners  trapped  in  a
collapsed mine through classical matched filter theory (i.e., the
cross-correlation  of  SOS  signals  with  pre-recorded  calibration
shots). This approach reveals a very interesting resolution poten-
tial  (<1  m  at  45  m  depth  below  ground  level)  and  performs
without the need of an exact velocity model, which is usually a
major concern in seismic event location. Matched filtering is not,
for obvious reasons, a suitable solution when dealing with micro-
seismic events triggered by survivors under rubble. Thus, a dif-
ferent path needs to be taken.

In  the  framework  of  a  university-industry  joint  project,  this
work is designed to improve S&R operations through the devel-
opment of a new system capable of semi-automatic detection and
location  of  microseismic  emissions  generated  by  survivors
trapped  under  rubble  (Arosio  2009).  At  present,  seismic  S&R
commercial systems rely solely on a subjective interpretation of
the energy of the sensed signals and disregard the potential infor-
mation related to propagation delays, although an array of trans-

ducers  is  usually  deployed.  A  time-consuming  iterative
move-and-listen procedure is needed to identify the sensor posi-
tion that returns the highest energy, approximately localizing the
projection  of  the  survivor  position  on  the  top  surface  of  the
debris  pile.  Traveltimes,  either  alone  or  integrated  with  energy
data,  can  extend  the  location  potential  of  present  S&R  seismic
equipment from a 2D to a 3D domain, provided that a properly
constrained acquisition geometry is employed (Arosio 2009). An
arrival-time-based approach may not represent the best solution
since automatic picking could be affected by wavelet instability
and  a  low  signal-to-roise  ratio  (S/N),  while  manual  picking
would not be feasible because of real-time constraints.
  Therefore, in this study I propose the use of a source location
algorithm  based  on  energy  focusing  of  the  waveforms  collected
along  the  surface  array  by  means  of  cross-correlation  and  sem-
blance operators. Although this approach does not require accurate
arrival time picks, information associated with a differential time
of arrival of the signal to the receivers is still essential and its con-
sistency with distance has to be acknowledged. Moreover, proper
signal preprocessing is needed in order to limit the effects of dis-
persion and obtain meaningful moveout corrections prior to focus-
ing.  In  the  following  sections,  I  describe  the  method  with  the
support  of  synthetic  examples  and  present  the  results  obtained
with real data sets collected in the three debris field scenarios.

mETHODOLOGY
Test sites
Three experimental tests were performed with firefighters at civil
protection training fields during the course of the project. Site 1
(Fig.  1a),  situated  in  Bovisio  Masciago  (Milan,  northern  Italy)
within the regional Fire Department training facilities, was used
for the sensor performance comparison (Arosio 2009). A linear
spread was deployed in five positions (Fig. 1a), both on grassy
areas  and  on  rubble  piles  consisting  of  concrete  beams,  while
shots  were  fired  from  three  positions  within  concrete  pipes
almost totally embedded in the ground. The canine search train-
ing  field  in  Luino  (Varese,  northern  Italy),  was  selected  as  the
second  test  site  (Fig.  1b).  Small  concrete  blocks  are  the  main
component  of  heterogeneous  debris,  which  also  consisted  of
rocks, soil and iron and covered a subsurface network of concrete
pipes located at a depth of about 1.5 m. In this site, a 12-channel
cross-spread  was kept in  the same  position throughout  the sur-
vey, while events were triggered in several positions at the sub-
surface (Fig. 1b), with the aim of testing the 2D and 3D location
results  according  to  different  source-array  relative  geometries.
Finally, tests were performed at site 3, i.e., within the headquar-
ters of the Pisa firefighters’ brigade. This site presented the most
difficult conditions since a very thick layer of extremely hetero-
geneous  rubble  covered  the  underground  pipe  network  used  to
simulate the presence of entombed people (Fig. 1c). Here, a quite
large  cross-spread  was  initially  deployed  with,  successively,
three  smaller  spreads  that  were  placed  in  relation  to  the  three
source positions (Fig. 1c), following a realistic search-and-locate

the field operators, usually located in the proximity of the evacuation
chimneys necessary for dog unit training. Source signals were cre-

the concrete tunnels were also collected. The amplitude spectra of
these sources roughly cover the 30–200 Hz range in sites 1 and 3 and
the 30–300 Hz range in site 2, with peaks typically placed around 80
Hz. Generally, data acquisitions were not time triggered in order to
simulate genuine survey conditions, except for a few shots fired with

elastic  signals  within  the  rubble.  Neglecting  the  extremely  high
velocities due to the significant influence of picking errors at short
offsets, the velocity values generally fell between 200–600 m/s.

In  order  to  compare  the  reliability  of  traveltime  and  energy
information, as gathered by each sensor along the deployed seis-
mic arrays, the arrival times were manually picked and the signal
energy computed within a window tuned to the events. Subsequently,
the sensor closest to the survivor was identified as the one with the
lowest traveltime or the highest peak energy. Errors in identifying
the  sensor  closest  to  the  position  of  the  operator  with  the  two
approaches  were  computed.  The  traveltime  analysis  resulted  in
fewer and generally lower errors. In some cases, the traveltime and
energy information may be considered as providing complemen-
tary results. In particular, no systematic dependence on source type
was observed in the trials. These results demonstrate that travel-
times can be combined with energy analysis to improve the statis-
tical reliability of locating the sensor that is closest to the survivor.
In addition, they encourage the research in the direction of explor-
ing algorithms for 3D location methods based on traveltime analy-
sis or joint traveltime-energy analysis.

Source location algorithm
The idea behind location by means of focalization comes from
the  velocity  analysis  of  seismic  data  (Yilmaz  2001). Assuming
that the position of the seismic source is in the three-dimension-
al subsurface and a homogeneous velocity for the elastic waves
(i.e., a linear raypath), the moveout of the signal is reconstructed
along the spread, channels are then statically corrected and the
level of likeness between them is computed. Repeating this pro-
cedure  for  different  source  points  and  velocities  yields  a
four-dimensional  matrix  containing  values  of  cross-correlation
changing in accordance with the ‘searching grid’. The maximum

  Since no information about the velocity model of the debris is
available,  it  was  deemed  reasonable  to  set  a  constant  velocity
throughout  the  investigated  area.  When  the  subsurface  model
lacks  sufficient  detail,  significant  errors  can  affect  trace  align-
ment  and,  as  a  consequence,  the  correct  identification  of  the
source position. This is particularly true when dealing with high-
frequency  components.  Hence,  a  low-pass  filtering  approach
should be considered when the model is simple. This processing
step has the additional advantage of moderating wavelet instabil-
ity  along  the  spread  caused  by  dispersion.  Reasonably,  as  the
frequency  content  of  the  input  signals  is  reduced  and  moved
towards  lower  frequencies,  one  would  expect  to  obtain  higher
levels  of  similarity  between  traces  with  the  cross-correlation/
semblance plots varying smoothly around the peak value (Arosio
et al. 2009). This leads to greater reliability in the location but
lower accuracy.
  Several methodologies may be exploited, either separately or
in combination, to lower the frequency content of the signal. In

this  study,  classical  digital  filtering  (Oppenheim  and  Schafer
1989), envelope computation (Taner et al. 1979) and the short-
time-average-to-long-time-average  (STA/LTA)  approach  (Lee
and Stewart 1981) were employed. In Fig. 3 a synthetic data set
is  perturbed  with  random  jitters  (i.e.,  time  shifts)  to  simulate
the effect of material heterogeneity with the jitters following a
normal  distribution  of  zero  mean  and  a  standard  deviation  of
5  ms.  The  5  ms  uncertainty  was  inferred  from  the  triggered
shots collected in the field and was calculated as the averaged
variation  of  traveltimes  taken  to  cover  similar  distances.  The
source  location  routine  is  performed  via  cross-correlation,
using  as  inputs:  the  raw  data,  100  Hz  low-pass  filtered  data,
data  envelope  approach  and  STA/LTA  computed  data  with  a
shorter window of 25 msec and a longer one spanning 50 msec
(note that a very low level noise was added to the data in order
to have a finite STA/LTA). The bandwidth of the 250 Hz domi-
nant frequency Ricker wavelet was too large to allow the iden-
tification of the correct source position (cf., Fig. 2) and the plot
of the cross-correlation trend in the azimuthal plane reveals low
values  and  a  number  of  small  local  maxima.  As  the  original
bandwidth is restricted and moved towards lower frequencies,
local peaks decrease in favour of augmented cross-correlation
values gently varying across the global peak. In this example,
the data envelope approach seems to provide the best trade-off
method  since  it  yields  a  satisfactory  cross-correlation  maxi-
mum value, an absence of distributed local peaks and a small
error (0.5 m) in the determination of the source position affect-
ing  the  x-coordinate  only.  Nevertheless,  effective  processing

RESULTS
This section will focus on data sets collected at test sites 2 and 3
that  provided  a  high  variability  of  source-sensor  distances  and
geometries, as well as relatively deeper source positions.
A preliminary location test was first performed with the raw data
in which the correct source hypocenter coordinates were given as
an input to the location algorithm (based on  cross-correlation).
The  medium’s  velocity  was  left  free  to  vary  at  50  m/sec  steps
across the range of 100–1500 m/sec. This trial assessed the good-
ness of the trace alignment and the value of the cross-correlation
maxima  when  using  a  homogeneous  model  in  place  of  a  more
complicated (actual) one. The results clearly demonstrate that the
crests  and  troughs  of  the  wavelets  in  the  traces  are  randomly
aligned, the output velocity varies according to the source type,
even  if  the  shots  are  fired  in  the  same  subsurface  position  and
that the cross-correlation values are extremely low (about 0.1),
indicative of a low reliability in the output (Fig. 5).
  Consequently,  it  was  decided  to  run  the  same  test  with  the
data  sets  processed  according  to  the  diverse  low-pass  filtering
methodologies indicated earlier. The degree of likeliness between
the traces as well as the goodness of trace alignment were com-
pared throughout the assessment in order to find a suitable and
robust  preprocessing  step  that  was  potentially  able  to  provide
reliable  location  outcomes.  The  adopted  filtering  approaches
consisted of i) plain low-pass filtering with high-cut frequencies

of both 50 Hz and 100 Hz (in relation to frequency content of the
events); ii) computation of the envelope of both raw events and
low-pass  filtered  data  sets;  iii)  calculation  of  trace  STA/LTA
using 300 ms and 100 ms to calculate the LTA and STA respec-
tively. Since solicited survivors tend to generate vibrations more
than once per second (approximate frequency ~2 Hz) and events
usually have a time duration of 100 ms (Firefighters Department,
pers.  comm.),  it  is  not  possible  to  set  the  LTA  window  signifi-
cantly  longer  than  the  STA  window  otherwise  the  LTA  will  be
altered by the presence of other events.

It  was  found  that  the  results  associated  with  both  low-pass
filtered data sets showed peak cross-correlation values that were
still too low (<0.25) in order to be satisfactory, hence plain filter-
ing is neither discussed here nor employed in the next 3D loca-
tion trials. However, the other filtering methodologies generally
yielded  peak  likelihood  values  ranging  across  the  0.75–0.95
interval and are therefore further investigated and compared.
  Location  tests  in  site  2  were  initially  performed  with  shots
generated by a hammer. The searching grid of the algorithm was
arbitrarily set as large as 6 m × 6 m × 4 m, was centred on the true
source position and was scanned with a 0.5 m step along all axes.

  The robustness of the location results relating to the position of
source B2 was then investigated according to source type, along
with energy of the shots generated. Although the cross-correlation
operator is not sensitive to differences in signal amplitude (i.e., in
signal energy), weak events may be fully corrupted by noise when
sensed  at  far-offsets  and  cause  a  random  alignment  of  collected
traces. Besides the preprocessing approaches, a new STA/LTA was
computed by shrinking both the STA and LTA windows to 25 ms
and  50  ms  respectively.  This  measure  was  intended  to  preserve
high frequencies with the aim of increasing location accuracy to
the  detriment  of  high  cross-correlation  values.  Obviously,  this
method  should  only  be  used  in  the  presence  of  high  S/N  traces
because it may be sensitive to noise spikes. Results from a detailed
analysis of shots fired at position B2 reveal that almost all the fil-
tering methodologies are able to provide an accuracy around 1m
and exhibit satisfactory reliability as far as source azimuthal loca-
tion is concerned. Determination of the source depth is, instead,
affected by larger errors due to the very limited development of the
sensing  array  along  the  z-axis.  This  aspect  has  a  considerable
impact on the semblance operator since the computation of trace
likeness is dominated by strong, short-offset events. According to
this,  the  robustness  of  the  azimuthal  location  may  be  increased
with  respect  to  the  cross-correlation  but  the  identification  of  the
correct source depth becomes less precise.
  Finally, larger-scale location tests were carried out by extend-
ing the searching grid of the algorithms to the whole debris field
(within an area of nearly 20 m × 20 m). Nevertheless, the results
still suggest that with a proper preprocessing method the location
accuracy of source position B2 remains within about 1m, whilst
the location accuracy and reliability of other source positions are
affected by random variations.

in site 2, a final thorough test was performed in site 3. A large
cross-spread with a geophone spacing of about 4 m was initially
deployed across the entire debris field over an area of approxi-

mately 20 m × 20 m. Automatic event detection along with zonal
source  location  algorithms  (Arosio  2009)  based  on  traveltime
and energy information were employed to estimate the 2D posi-
tion of generated emissions. Subsequently, smaller spreads with
reduced  sensor  inter-distances  (~1.5  m)  were  centred  on  three
identified  source  areas  (cf.,  Fig.  1c)  and  3D  location  was
attempted.  For  completeness,  the  data  collected  with  the  large
array were 3D-focused after the searching grid was set within a
box of 6 m × 6 m × 4 m surrounding the identified source azi-

the  findings  are  consistent  with  the  acquisition  geometry.  The
accuracy of the location is very satisfactory for S2, placed close
to the centre of the spread, whilst positions S1 and S5 generally
display higher errors. S3 and S4 have the maximum errors. Low
semblance values for the S2 source are due to large variations in
signal amplitude along the traces collected within the large array.
The 3D location performed on the smaller spread data sets gener-
ally provided results with errors in the order of the seismic reso-
lution, i.e., ≤2 m. Location was performed both within the previ-
ously defined searching grid centred on each source position and
with searching parameters defined by spread boundaries (at least
as far as x- and y-coordinates are concerned). No significant dif-
ferences were obtained. Since all of the arrays deployed at site 3
were  nearly  arranged  on  a  sub-horizontal  plane,  an  effective
evaluation  of  the  location  outputs  in  the  depth  axis  was  once
again prevented.

DISCUSSION
The  location  results  reported  earlier  are  consistent  with  the  fact
that  there  is  little  opportunity  to  precisely  locate  a  microseismic
event  with  a  badly-conditioned  transducer  array  geometry.
Receivers  should  surround  the  area  containing  the  expected
sources and the array should be deployed in manner so that it is not
dimension-reduced, i.e., sensor arrays should be distributed in the
monitored area along all directions (Ge and Hardy 1988; Eisner et
al.  2009; Arosio  2009).  Besides  having  a  favourable  array  geo-
metry, sources close to the centre of the spread are expected to be
located with a satisfactory precision because of a higher S/N.

    According  to  this,  an  optimal  searching  strategy  was  devel-
oped with initially a large cross-spread with geophone spacings
of no more than 5 m deployed across the rubble pile. Acquisition
is then manually triggered after the survivor response has been
solicited  by  the  S&R  operators.  Collected  data  are  then  proc-
essed and, in case of event detection, zonal location is performed
and the source is roughly located in the azimuthal plane. At this
stage, a search supervisor can, depending on both the identified
source  area  and  the  quality  of  recorded  traces,  decide  either  to
perform 3D data focusing or to shift and shrink the cross-spread
and resume the entire procedure. In the former case, 3D location
is performed within a limited searching grid centred on the iden-
tified source area, whilst in the latter case the area to be explored
is limited by the spread boundaries. Either the spread has, or has
not, been repositioned before starting the 3D focusing procedure.
The  positions  of  transducers  are  automatically  determined  by
means  of  an  auto-location  process  (Bernasconi  and  Rovetta
2009)  and  coordinates  obtained  serve  as  inputs  for  performing
meaningful static corrections. Our experience in the field demon-
strated that a trained 5-person crew can accomplish the complete
searching procedure in less than 15 minutes.
  The  comparable  location  accuracies  of  the  cross-correlation
and  semblance  approaches  (despite  the  different  normalization
factors) can be explained because once the spread has been cen-
tred on a source position, related records will feature good S/N
along nearly all channels. This limits the influence of noisy chan-
nels  on  cross-correlation  and  the  domination  of  a  few  high-
energy  traces  within  the  semblance  computation. Although  the
semblance operator sometimes displayed lower vertical accuracy
with respect to the cross-correlation, an accurate comparison was
not possible because all the employed spreads lacked a vertical
component.  Considering  that  remarkable  variations  in  channel
energies may occur in the field, despite favourable source-spread
relative position, the different features of the two operators could
be combined in sequence to improve the location procedure with
an  acceptable  increase  of  computational  costs.  Being  strongly
influenced  by  short-offset  channels,  semblance  could  be  used
first  to  obtain  a  reliable  estimate  of  source  azimuthal  position.
Then,  cross-correlation  can  be  evaluated  over  a  constrained
searching grid constrained by previously defined epicentre coor-
dinates.  In  fact,  as  the  operator  is  more  sensitive  to  far-offset
channels, it can provide a better estimate of the source depth.

In keeping with earlier findings, the efficiency of the diverse
filtering methodologies was evaluated considering only the hori-
zontal accuracy of results. Since no significant differences were
observed throughout the trials, the computation of the envelope
of both raw and filtered data sets, as well as the computation of
the  STA/LTA,  may  be  employed  as  useful  methodologies  for
lowering  the  frequency  content  of  the  collected  traces  whilst
preserving information associated with arrival times and energy.
Intuitively, a preprocessing step involving filtering may be more
reliable because it has the capability to reject unwanted signals
falling outside the bandwidth of interest. On the other hand, plain

STA/LTA and envelope calculation may be processing steps that
are not as robust as filtering. For instance, evidence in the field
has  shown  that  a  spurious  signal  collected  by  a  receiver  just
before  an  event  generated  by  a  trapped  person  can  modify  the
trend either of the STA/LTA ratio or of the envelope and affect
the  differential  time  of  arrival  information.  Of  course,  in  order
for low-pass filtering to be effective, the cut-off frequency should
ideally be tuned according to the specific characteristics of the
investigated sites. Experimental tests carried out in three differ-
ent debris fields suggested that the frequency content of collected
events is centred around 100 Hz and this has allowed the imple-
mentation of a sub-optimal preprocessing sequence.

CONCLUSIONS
The reliable location of survivors trapped in the wreckage debris
of a disaster is a very challenging task and diverse indirect tech-
niques  are  now  being  employed  by  S&R  teams  to  achieve  this
goal. Microseismic monitoring is a methodology borrowed from
the geophysical field that may prove to be helpful in the detec-
tion and location of entombed people that are generating elastic
signals travelling through the rubble. Though already employed
in  first-emergency  management  operations,  this  technique  still
needs improvement to best fit this niche application and to pro-
vide  task  force  personnel  with  accurate  and  reliable  results.  In
fact, current microseismic approaches to S&R issues rely on the
subjective  interpretation  of  event  energy  and  can  only  provide
S&R personnel with an approximate azimuthal (2D) position of
the survivor.
  During field tests it was understood that traveltimes seem to
carry information about the survivor location that is as reliable as
energy-related data despite the heterogeneity of the propagation
medium;  this  has  encouraged  the  development  of  2D  and  3D
location algorithms based on traveltime analysis or joint travel-
time-energy analysis. In this study, the issue of 3D location was
addressed with cross-correlation as well as semblance operators.
Both  operators  produced  comparable  location  results  within  a
seismic resolution limit (accuracy ≤2 m), given that proper acqui-
sition geometry and an effective filtering method were employed.
On the basis of these outcomes, an effective searching strategy
was established involving event detection in 2D along with 3D in
sequence.  This  procedure  allows  the  monitoring  of  a  20  m  ×
20 m area in approximately 15 minutes, reducing the investiga-
tion time taken by current seismic S&R systems by a factor of
three. Yet,  no  single  searching  methodology  should  be  consid-
ered  sufficiently  effective  enough  to  ensure  that  a  complete
search  has  been  conducted.  The  most  effective  search  strategy
should blend all viable tactical capabilities into a logical plan of
operations.
  A  meaningful  assessment  of  location  accuracy  along  the
depth  axis  was  prevented  because  of  the  limited  extent  of  the
deployed  spreads  in  the  vertical  direction.  Future  work  will
involve field trials with arrays distributed in the monitored area
along  all  directions  and  the  evaluation  of  location  accuracy
