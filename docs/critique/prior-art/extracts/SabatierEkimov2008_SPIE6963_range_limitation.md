Range limitation for seismic footstep detection
James M. Sabatier ∗ and Alexander E. Ekimov
The University of Mississippi
National Center for Physical Acoustics
1 Coliseum Drive, University, MS USA 38677
ABSTRACT
Seismic methods for footstep detection exploit low frequency vibration waves, typically below 100 Hz. There are two
limiting factors for detection of human footsteps at these frequencies: walking styles and the background noise floor.
The walking style changes the dynamic footstep force on the ground and, therefore, limits the maximum distance at
which walkers may be detected. For seismic frequencies, the background vibration noise floor is higher in urban areas
than in quiet areas. This article presents and discusses test results of human footstep measurements as a function of
distance using the seismic method in quiet and urban areas.
Keywords: Footstep, seismic, background noise, band-pass filtering, walking styles
1. INTRODUCTION
Human footsteps excite envelopes of broadband seismic signals in the ground due to impulsive dynamic forces from
footsteps.1-5 These envelopes of seismic signals form a periodic pulse sequence that has a characteristic time T (the
footstep repetition time) that is opposite to the repetition frequency or footstep rate, which usually lies below 4 Hz.
Seismic signals from a walker are naturally filtered by the ground similar to a band-pass filter6 with a center frequency
within the frequency band of 20-90 Hz and attenuate over distance by geometric spreading losses due to propagation and
energy losses from absorption.5
There are two factors limiting the range of detection for footstep vibrations: the background vibration noise floor and
walking styles. The background vibration noise floor is higher in urban areas than in quiet (e.g. rural) areas. According
to Ref. 7, the typical background RMS vibration noise corresponds to 8 x 10-6 m/s or 50 dB re: 10-6 inches/sec. For
reference, the peak magnitudes of seismic vibrations from human footsteps did not exceed 3 x 10-6 m/s, even very close
(3 meters) to the walker.8 This is less than the typical level of the background vibration noise floor and indicates a
possible inability to apply the seismic method for footstep detection in urban areas, which are characterized by higher
than 50 dB levels of background noise.
The footstep dynamic forces have a strong dependence on the walking styles employed (standard, soft, or stealthy) as
shown in Refs. 3-5,9. Tests results presented in Ref. 3 demonstrate that, for walkers 1 meter from a seismic sensor, soft
and stealthy walking styles reduced the vibration response of the ground by 7.5 dB and 12.5 dB, respectively, relative to
regular walking. As a result, the distance range for footstep detection was reduced for soft and stealthy styles compared
to the regular walking style at the same background noise floor level.
Test results of human footstep measurements using the seismic method with respect to the distance in quiet and urban
areas and walking styles are presented and discussed in this article.
2. MEASUREMENTS OF HUMAN FOOTSTEPS IN QUIET AND URBAN AREAS
Seismic signals from human footsteps were measured on the ground during daytime hours on the University of
Mississippi (UM) campus, where the background noise floor was equal to or higher than the typical level of 50 dB, and
in a quiet area at the Army Research Laboratory’s Blossom Point facility (BP).

3. BACKGROUND VIBRATION NOISE FLOOR
The background vibration noise floor is used to characterize areas as quiet or urban. The characteristic typical
background vibration noise level of 50 dB re: 10-6 inches/sec, which corresponds to 8 x 10-6 m/s, was presented in Ref.7.
The vibrations due to different urban (transport and construction) activities above that level were measured and
classified systematically.7 Also, the noise level is highest at low frequencies and rolls off with increasing frequency. In
practice, the frequency band of 1-100 Hz is normally used for ground vibration measurements10. For footstep vibrations,
the frequencies below 100 Hz are used, typically within narrow frequency band (e.g.10-40 Hz1 or 5-50 Hz8).

UM campus. In these measurements, the sampling rate was 96 kHz. A geophone, GS-32CT, measured the noise floor at
in the BP area. The seismic signals from the geophone were digitized using a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz. In Fig. 2, the

4. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS OF FOOTSTEP SEISMIC SIGNAL DETECTION
4.1. Footstep signals in an urban area (the UM campus)
From the data presented in Tab.1, band-pass filtering of the background noise reduced the noise floor and potentially
improved the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The relationship between the footstep signals and noise vibrations within a
narrow frequency band depends on the site-specific transfer function, which has a maximum within the frequency band
of 20-90 Hz.6 As shown in Ref. 6, the footstep spectra for different outdoor sites have maxima at different frequencies.
As the maximum of the site transfer function is not known a priori, a parallel signal processing algorithm was applied to
the footstep signals to improve the SNR and increase the distance of footstep detection. A block diagram of this
algorithm is shown in Fig. 3. Recorded footstep signals were band-pass filtered with five narrow-band filters. For the
reference, a relatively broadband band-pass filter (BPF) with width of 30 Hz (from 10-40 Hz1) was used. Four other
band-pass filters had a width of 10 Hz. The measured data were processed with a 0.185 s sliding Hanning window. This
time is approximately equal to the time duration of a single footstep, that is defined as the time interval for a single
footstep from “heel strike” to “toe slap and weight transfer”.9 The RMS vibration levels were calculated for each of the
filtered signals with a 75% overlap by root square summation of RMS signals within of BPF.
Footstep seismic signals for regular, soft, and stealthy walking styles were recorded using the setup shown in Fig. 1. The
same walking speed and with the same person were used to exclude the personal difference parameter from the inquiry.
A DAQ with a sampling rate of 88.2 kHz and 44.1 kHz anti-aliasing filter acquired signals from the accelerometer.
The spectrograms and corresponding RMS vibration levels of band-pass filtered signals for regular, soft, and stealthy
footsteps are presented in Figs. 4(a)-4(f), Figs. 5(a)-5(f), and in Figs. 6(a)-6(f), respectively. In the spectrograms [Figs.
4(a)-6(a)], the footstep vibrations are presented in the frequency band of 10- 80 Hz for all walking styles since the
vibrations for higher frequencies were less detectable. The FFT size was 16,384 in the spectrograms in Figs. 4(a)-6(a).
This FFT size corresponds to 5.383 Hz in the spectral line resolution and 185.76 ms in the time resolution. At the top of
the spectrograms, the directions of walking and distance range are shown. A person started walking 1 meter from the
accelerometer and stopped walking at 17 meters from this sensor location. 19 steps were required to complete a distance
of 16 meters.

Seismic signals were recorded using the setup shown in Fig.1 and processed using the algorithm shown in Fig.3 for

a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz and 22.05 kHz anti-aliasing filter acquired signals from the geophone.
The spectrograms and corresponding RMS vibration levels of band-pass filtered signals of regular and stealthy footsteps
are presented in Figs. 8(a) - 8(f) and Figs. 9(a) - 9(f), respectively. In the spectrograms [Figs. 8(a)-9(a)], the footstep

5. SUMMARY
Human footstep seismic signals for three distinct styles of walking (regular, soft, and stealthy) were measured on an
outdoor ground in quiet and urban areas. These areas were characterized by different background vibration noise floor
levels. In the frequency band of 5-50 Hz, the average value of background vibrations for the quiet area was 37 dB less
than for the urban area.
Band-pass filtering of the background noise reduced the noise floor and improved SNR. The relationship between the
footstep signals and noise vibrations within narrow frequency bands depends on the site-specific transfer function, which
has a maximum within the frequency band of 20-90 Hz.6 As the maximum of the site transfer function is not known a
priori, a parallel signal processing algorithm for footstep signals was used that improved the SNR and increased the
distance of footstep detection.
The results indicated a strong dependence between distances of footstep detection and the background noise floor and
walking styles. Vibrations from the regular walking style were detected in the frequency band of 30-40 Hz at a distance
of 17 m in the urban area and at the distance of 94 m in the frequency bands of 10-40 Hz and 10-20 Hz in the quiet area.
Soft and stealthy footstep vibrations were detected at distances of 12 m and 5 m, respectively, in the urban area. Stealthy
footsteps were detected at about 52 m from a walker in the quiet area.
